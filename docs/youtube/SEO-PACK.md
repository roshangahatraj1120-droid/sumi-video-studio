**Thumbnail text:** WHO DANCED FIRST? (cave hand-prints and fire, your face looking curious)

**Description**
```
When did humans start dancing, and why? Before stages, studios and choreographers, humans moved. In this documentary I follow the evidence for the origin of dance across thousands of years: from rock art in Africa to ancient Egypt, Greece, India, Europe and finally my home, Nepal.

There is no recording of the first dance, no fossil and no ancient document that says "this person was a dancer". Dance is movement, and movement disappears. So instead of pretending to know the first dance, we follow the clues: rock art linked with San healing dances, Egyptian clappers used to keep time for dance, the Maenads of Dionysus, the Natya Shastra and its 108 karanas, Lord Shiva as Nataraja, and the moment Europe began writing dance down in the 17th century.

Along the way the question changes. It is no longer only "when did humans start dancing?" but "what has dance carried all the way to today?" Stories, beliefs, tradition, memory and identity.

This is the first episode of "Dance: Where We Come From", a documentary series about the history of dance, the cultures behind it and the people who keep it alive.

People say hip-hop was born on August 11, 1973: one party, one building, one DJ, one night. But culture doesn't work like that. If hip-hop was born that day, what was happening the day before?

## 3 · Rudolf Laban: The Dance Alphabet That Reached the Pentagon (10:43) · Sat Nov 21, 6:00 PM NPT

**Titles**
- ⭐ The Dance Alphabet That Reached the Pentagon | Rudolf Laban's Strange Legacy
- Rudolf Laban: How Dance Notation Ended Up in Factories, Corporations & the Pentagon
- He Invented a Written Language for Dance. Then It Was Used for Power.

**Thumbnail text:** DANCE → PENTAGON? (Laban's portrait, a notation score and a red classified stamp)

**Description**
```
In 1928, Rudolf Laban published an idea that sounds almost impossible: a written language for human movement. Music could be written down. Words could be written down. But dance disappears the moment the body stops moving. Laban's answer was an alphabet, not for speech but for the body. This is the story of what happened when human movement became information.

We follow Labanotation (Kinetography Laban) from European modern dance into some of the strangest corners of the 20th century: Laban's uncomfortable career inside Nazi-era cultural institutions and the cancelled 1936 Olympic production, wartime British factories and Laban–Lawrence Industrial Rhythm, the Dance Notation Bureau in New York, and Hanya Holm's "Kiss Me, Kate", described by the Library of Congress as the first copyright registration claim for a choreographic work.

Then the story gets stranger. Warren Lamb's Movement Pattern Analysis moved into corporate hiring and management consulting, and according to published research, movement analysis was later used in a Pentagon Office of Net Assessment project on decision-making style. But the same ideas also moved toward healing, through Irmgard Bartenieff's work with polio patients and therapy, and toward culture, through Alan Lomax's choreometrics.

The same alphabet can write a love letter or a military order. It was never the language that decided. It was the people who used it.

As a child, the Dashain mela at my mama ghar in Sharanamati, Jhapa, meant jalebi, langur burja, a little money to spend, and the sound of the Santhal drums. This year I came back with a different curiosity: not just to watch, but to learn, to listen and to understand.

We also meet the next generation, young dancers performing alongside older members, and finally I join the line myself. The same dance I watched with so much excitement as a child, I was now dancing alongside them.

Culture doesn't survive through memories alone. It survives when people continue to practise it, when stories are shared, and when younger generations get the chance to learn from those who came before them.
